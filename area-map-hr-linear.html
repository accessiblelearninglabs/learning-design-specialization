Section 1

About this branch

Education about, through and for human rights, as set out in the United Nations Declaration on Human Rights Education and Training. This branch gathers the contributions that turn instruments into practice: the right to education itself as a design framework, the established models of human rights education and what each is for, the move from knowing a right to being able to invoke it, and the conditions under which serious harm can be taught well. It is the branch the accessibility and equity work draws its legal grounding from.

Grounding. United Nations Declaration on Human Rights Education and Training; the right to education (Tomaševski; CESCR General Comment 13); models of human rights education (Tibbitts; Bajaj)

The Right to Education Itself

The anchor · available, accessible, acceptable, adaptable

Planned

The 4-A scheme developed by the United Nations Special Rapporteur on the right to education, and affirmed in General Comment 13, sets out what states owe: that education exists, that people can reach it, that its form and content are acceptable to those receiving it, and that it adapts to their circumstances. Read as a design framework it gives learning design a vocabulary drawn from international law for questions it already asks, and connects each to an established obligation.

Grounding. 4-A scheme (Tomaševski); CESCR General Comment 13; UDHR Article 26; ICESCR Article 13; CRC Articles 28–29; CRPD Article 24

Models of Human Rights Education

Awareness, accountability, transformation — and choosing between them

Planned

Three widely used models describe what a human rights education programme is actually for: building shared values and awareness, equipping professionals to meet obligations they already hold, and supporting people affected by rights violations to act. Each implies different learners, content and evidence of success. Identifying which model a request belongs to, early, is what allows a programme to be designed and evaluated coherently.

Grounding. Models of human rights education (Tibbitts); ideology, location and approaches (Bajaj); World Programme for Human Rights Education

Sources

  • Reference 7: Tibbitts, F. (2002). “Understanding What We Do: Emerging Models for Human Rights Education.” International Review of Education, 48(3–4), 159–171.

Rights That Can Be Used, Not Only Known

From the instrument to the claim a person can make

Planned

Knowing that a right exists and being able to invoke it are separate capabilities, and the second is where most programmes have room to grow. The design work is concrete: which body receives the claim, what a complaint contains, what timelines apply, what support exists, and rehearsal of the request itself — so that the learning ends closer to action than to awareness.

Grounding. Education for human rights (UN Declaration on Human Rights Education and Training); transformative agency (Bajaj); critical pedagogy (Freire)

Sources

  • Reference 10: United Nations General Assembly (2011). United Nations Declaration on Human Rights Education and Training. Resolution 66/137, A/RES/66/137, 19 December 2011.

Teaching About Serious Harm

Content whose subject is present in the room

Planned

Some rights content concerns events that participants, their families or their communities have lived through, and the alternative to teaching it carries its own costs. The design commitments are stated in advance and hold throughout: what will be covered and when, pacing that can be adjusted, participation that does not require anyone to account for their own experience, and a route out of the room that costs nothing to use.

Grounding. Difficult knowledge (Britzman); pedagogy of discomfort (Boler); trauma-informed educational practice (Carello & Butler); SAMHSA framework

Sources

Rights Education Inside the Duty-Bearing Institution

When the organization teaching the right also owes it

Planned

Workplace and institutional rights education is usually delivered by the body that holds the corresponding obligation. Designing it well means naming that position openly, giving accurate routes to internal and external recourse including those outside the organization, and separating the record of who attended from anything that could identify what an individual disclosed.

Grounding. Duty-bearer obligations (UN Declaration on Human Rights Education and Training); human rights codes and complaint procedures; privacy in learning records

Sources

Human Rights in Public Interpretation

Rights content in a gallery, a memorial or a public programme

Planned

Public settings reach audiences that no course reaches, and increasingly carry rights content: contested collections, memorials, and exhibitions on histories of harm. The interpretive questions are who narrates, what the visitor is told about provenance, and how a self-directed visitor of any age and background is supported through material of this weight in a short, unsupervised encounter.

A resource in two areas. Its home is Human Rights Education, and it is also part of the Public Learning set.

Grounding. Difficult heritage (Macdonald); interpretation (Serrell); Calls to Action (TRC); museum education (Hooper-Greenhill)

Selection, Identification and the Right to Education

How places are allocated, and who is identified for them

Planned

Streaming, enrichment placement, admissions screens and eligibility criteria distribute educational opportunity, and the acceptability and adaptability obligations apply directly to them. Documenting the criterion actually used, who is identified by it and who is not, and what alternative routes exist, brings selection into the same evidentiary frame as any other design decision.

Grounding. 4-A scheme (Tomaševski); CESCR General Comment 13; construct validity (Messick); equity evaluation (Mertens)

Sources
