Section 1

About the trunk

The shared craft of learning design, which every branch grows from and none of them owns. It runs from the first diagnostic question — whether this is a learning problem at all — through scoping, working with experts, writing, visual language and production, and out to the practices the field holds in common: quality assurance, source discipline, privacy, ethics, update cycles, and the case made for the resource in the first place.

The trunk is not a sixth specialization. It is what the five branches — Learning Experience Design, Learning Systems Design, Knowledge Mobilization, Universal Design for Learning, and Accessibility — all draw on and none of them owns.

Telling a Learning Need from a Performance Problem

The question asked before anything is designed

Planned

A request for training usually arrives attached to a problem training cannot fix — unclear expectations, missing tools, broken incentives, understaffing, a marking load nobody costed, a prerequisite that quietly stopped being taught. Diagnosing which is which, before any design begins, changes the outcome more than any later decision will. It is also the step most often skipped.

Grounding. Behaviour engineering (Gilbert); performance analysis (Mager & Pipe); Rummler & Brache

Scoping and the Decision Gate

Correcting while correction is still cheap

Planned

Designs get built well past the point where changing them stops being affordable. A named milestone, a defined output, and a structured reassessment before more is committed — whether what is being committed is a budget or someone’s unpaid hours — is what keeps a project correctable rather than merely finishable.

Grounding. Project scoping and staged decision-making

Working with Subject-Matter Experts

Surfacing what an expert can no longer see

Planned

Drawing out tacit knowledge, running review cycles that converge, and holding design integrity when the expert is also the partner who approves the work. Experts forget what was once hard for them, and cannot tell you what they have stopped noticing. Getting that knowledge out takes a deliberate method, not a good conversation.

Onboarding a Partner into Learning Design

How someone learns to commission this work

Planned

Few partners know what to ask a learning designer for, what they will be asked to supply, or what good looks like when it arrives. That holds for an organization with a procurement process and for a single person with a course, a deadline, and no budget line at all. The intake, the shared vocabulary, and the first engagement are design problems in their own right.

Minimum Viable Content

Deciding what to cut, before anything is built

Planned

The instinct to cover everything is the most reliable source of bloated, unfinishable learning. Deciding what genuinely must be present is an editorial judgment made before design begins — distinct from cognitive load, which is a limit on what working memory can hold once material is in front of someone.

Grounding. Editorial scoping before design; cognitive load (Sweller)

Sources

Planned

What the tool is good for, and what it invents

Planned

This stays about generative tools in the designer’s hands. Their use by learners, and what that does to assessment, is a separate contribution.

What to Build When the Answer Isn’t a Course

Support placed at the moment the work happens

Planned

Job aids, checklists, decision aids at the point of care, protocol cards, a worked-example set, a rubric released before the work starts, a clearer brief, a page that answers the question forty people are about to ask. Support available at the moment the task is performed rather than instruction delivered weeks earlier and expected to survive until it is needed — frequently where the measurable effect actually is.

Visual Language and Design Systems for Learning Programs

Making consistency economical instead of heroic

Planned

Materials produced by many hands drift — inconsistent, off-brand, and inaccessible in the same recurring ways. Templates, type and colour decisions with contrast decided once, a media library, and a house style are what let quality hold without a specialist reviewing every artefact.

This is what makes facilitation craft and design infrastructure work. Without it they are aspirations.

Grounding. Design systems practice; WCAG contrast requirements

Plain Language and Writing for Learning

Reading load as a design decision

Planned

How hard something is to read is usually the biggest barrier to understanding it, and it has nothing to do with the subject itself. It is also the barrier designers most often leave to chance. Reading load decides whether a patient follows their discharge instructions, whether a public notice meets its plain-language duty, whether a researcher can actually use a method someone has described, and whether a student can tell what an assignment is asking them to do.

Grounding. ISO 24495-1; health literacy (Nutbeam)

How Learning Works, and Who Can Act on It

Told plainly to learners, and built into the design

Planned

Most learners have never been told how consolidation works, why re-reading feels productive and isn’t, or that difficulty is often the signal rather than the failure. This makes that knowledge available in plain language as something offered rather than assigned, and builds the regulating into the design — spacing in the schedule, retrieval in the activity, requirements stated plainly — because every strategy in this literature presupposes conditions that unequal circumstances do not distribute equally.

Grounding. Learning How to Learn (Oakley & Sejnowski); desirable difficulties (Bjork); retrieval practice (Roediger & Karpicke); co-regulated and socially shared regulation (Hadwin & Järvelä); scarcity and bandwidth (Mullainathan & Shafir)

Sources

  • Reference 22: Bjork, R. A., & Bjork, E. L. (2011). “Making Things Hard on Yourself, but in a Good Way: Creating Desirable Difficulties to Enhance Learning.” In Psychology and the Real World. Worth Publishers.
  • Reference 23: Roediger, H. L., & Karpicke, J. D. (2006). “Test-Enhanced Learning: Taking Memory Tests Improves Long-Term Retention.” Psychological Science, 17(3), 249–255.
  • Reference 24: Hadwin, A. F., Järvelä, S., & Miller, M. (2018). “Self-Regulation, Co-Regulation, and Shared Regulation in Collaborative Learning Environments.” In Handbook of Self-Regulation of Learning and Performance (2nd ed.). Routledge.
  • Reference 25: Mullainathan, S., & Shafir, E. (2013). Scarcity: Why Having Too Little Means So Much. Times Books.

Writing Outcomes That Can Be Assessed

Holding outcome, evidence and activity in one line

Planned

Most outcomes state an aspiration no assessment can reach — appreciate, understand, be aware of — and most activities drift from whatever is finally marked. Naming what someone will be able to do, choosing the evidence that would show it, and building the activity that gets them there is the most-performed move in the craft and the one most often made by habit. It is also what makes a design reviewable by anyone who was not in the room when it was decided.

Backward design on the experience branch applies this to one designed experience. Here it is the general move, applied to anything that claims to teach something.

Grounding. Constructive alignment (Biggs); backward design (Wiggins & McTighe); construct validity (Messick)

Sources

  • Reference 27: Biggs, J. (1996). “Enhancing Teaching Through Constructive Alignment.” Higher Education, 32(3), 347–364.

Assessment When the Answer Can Be Generated

Designing tasks a model cannot sit in for

Planned

Detection is unreliable, adversarial, and falls hardest on the people least able to contest it. The durable answers are in the task itself: work anchored to a particular context, a process that leaves a trail, evidence gathered in person at the points where it genuinely matters, and stated expectations about permitted use rather than a blanket prohibition nobody can enforce. The prior question is which capability a task is protecting, and whether it is worth protecting there.

Grounding. Assessment security (Dawson); permitted-use scales (Perkins et al.); construct validity (Messick)

Sources

  • Reference 31: Dawson, P. (2021). Defending Assessment Security in a Digital World: Preventing E-Cheating and Supporting Academic Integrity in Higher Education. Routledge.

When the Consultation Is the Whole Intervention

One conversation, and no project behind it

Planned

A great deal of this work is not a project. It is an hour with someone who has a course, a deadline, and the option of simply not writing back. What can be diagnosed, decided, and left behind in a single conversation is a different craft from running a scoped build, and it is where most of a practice’s actual reach lives. Designing that hour — what to ask, what to skip, what to leave in their hands — deserves the same attention as designing anything else.

Distinct from onboarding a partner, which assumes an engagement follows. This one assumes it may not.

Grounding. Reflective practice (Schön); needs analysis (Rossett); adult learning (Knowles)

Sources

  • Reference 33: Schön, D. A. (1983). The Reflective Practitioner: How Professionals Think in Action. Basic Books.

Reading Learning Science Without Neuromyths

Telling the evidence from the folklore

Planned

Learning styles, the retention pyramid, left-brain and right-brain, and the attention-span figures that circulate without a source. Supporting Indigenous-Led Learning Design

A settler designer’s role, named at its actual size

Planned

Design craft placed in service of Indigenous-led education and directed by Indigenous knowledge holders — never a claim to Indigenous knowledge, which is held in relationship and community and does not transfer to a portfolio. The scholarship belongs to Ramsden, Kirkness and Barnhardt, Archibald, Battiste, Ermine, and Tuhiwai Smith; what is offered here is process, plain language, accessibility, and production discipline in support of work whose direction is not the designer’s to set.

Cultural safety is determined by the person receiving the care or the teaching, never asserted by the person providing it — Ramsden’s principle, from the origin of the concept in Māori nursing scholarship. A designer can work toward an environment being safer, and cannot certify it safe on someone else’s behalf. This contribution describes contribution, not authority: it says what the design craft supplied and under whose direction.

Grounding. Cultural safety (Ramsden); the Four R’s — respect, relevance, reciprocity, responsibility (Kirkness & Barnhardt); Indigenous Storywork (Archibald); ethical space of engagement (Ermine); Decolonization Is Not a Metaphor (Tuck & Yang). Instruments: TRC Calls to Action 23 and 24; UNDRIP; BC’s Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples Act; the First Nations principles of OCAP®; FNHA cultural safety and humility commitments.

Reading Course Signals Without Overreading Them

Feedback, evaluations, and platform data

Planned

Learner feedback, end-of-term evaluation instruments, and platform analytics each answer a narrower question than they appear to. Evaluation instruments carry documented bias by instructor gender; platform data records activity rather than learning; open comments are a self-selected sample in which the unhappy are overrepresented. Designing Inside a Research Culture

No authority, and a partner whose first obligation is elsewhere

Planned

In institutions built around producing knowledge, the person being designed with is an expert in their own field first, holds more standing in it than the designer does, and is taking part voluntarily. Nothing can be required. The evidence has to meet the standard they apply to their own discipline, which is higher than the one most learning content is written to. And the design has to survive a term in which teaching is one obligation of three. The craft is influence without authority, and evidence good enough for a sceptical expert.

Distinct from working with subject-matter experts, which is about eliciting what they know. This is about the conditions under which they can be worked with at all.

Grounding. Adult learning (Knowles); expert blind spot (Nathan & Petrosino); evidence use (Nutley)
