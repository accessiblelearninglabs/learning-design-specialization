Section 1

About this branch

Learning designed for people who are not enrolled: visitors, community members, practitioners outside the academy, and anyone who arrives voluntarily and may leave at any moment. This branch gathers the contributions that address the encounter itself — attention, groups, objects, interpretation, and the public room — alongside the infrastructure that makes public learning possible, from access to the research literature to community-held records. Museums, science centres, galleries, libraries and public programmes are its settings.

Grounding. Free-choice learning; museum education; visitor studies; open access; public library and information literacy practice

Learning Nobody Is Required to Do

The entry node · attention as the working budget

Planned

Without enrolment, completion or consequence for leaving, continuation is granted moment by moment and every element has to earn it again. Studies of visitor time establish that most people spend well under half an hour in an exhibition and stop at a minority of what is available, which sets an honest ceiling on what a single encounter can be designed to achieve and directs the design toward interest, orientation and a reason to return.

Grounding. Free-choice learning (Falk & Dierking); visitor time and attention (Serrell); Learning Science in Informal Environments (NRC)

Designing for Groups Who Arrive Together

The conversation is where the learning travels

Planned

Families, school groups and pairs of friends arrive as a unit, and the accompanying adult interprets while also learning. Research on museum conversation shows that what people say to each other in front of an exhibit is a principal route through which understanding forms, which makes the design object the exchange rather than the individual: prompts that give an adult something to ask, and content that supports several reading levels at one label.

Grounding. Learning conversations in museums (Leinhardt, Crowley & Knutson); sociocultural learning (Vygotsky); free-choice learning

Sources

  • Reference 5: Leinhardt, G., Crowley, K., & Knutson, K. (Eds.). (2002). Learning Conversations in Museums. Lawrence Erlbaum Associates.

Interpretation Bound to an Object and Its Provenance

What the collection holds, and how it came to be held

Planned

In collection-based settings the content available is the content the institution holds, and the history of how each object arrived is part of what the label is answering for. Interpretation is therefore developed with the communities to whom the material belongs and whose knowledge it carries, with authorship, protocol and the terms of display settled alongside the text rather than after it.

Grounding. Interpretation (Serrell); difficult heritage (Macdonald); ethical space (Ermine); OCAP® principles

Sources

Making the Case for Public Learning Outcomes

Interest, identity and participation, evidenced

Planned

Public learning produces outcomes that arrive slowly and diffusely — interest, confidence, a sense that a field is open to the person standing in it — while funding decisions often expect the shorter, school-shaped kind. The strands of informal science learning set out in the National Research Council synthesis give these outcomes a documented structure, which supports proposals and reports that describe what public programmes genuinely do and evidence it on its own terms.

Grounding. Strands of informal science learning (NRC); free-choice learning; evaluation of public programmes

Sources

  • Reference 3: National Research Council (2009). Learning Science in Informal Environments: People, Places, and Pursuits. National Academies Press.

Delivery by Volunteers and Casual Teams

Designed to hold in the hands of a short-notice team

Planned

Much public programming is delivered by volunteers, students and casual staff who join for a season and receive a short orientation. The design response is to decide deliberately what is fixed and what is left to the person: a small number of well-rehearsed moves, prompts and worked examples in hand, and a clear account of what to do when a question runs past the brief.

Grounding. Legitimate peripheral participation (Lave & Wenger); job aids and performance support (Rossett & Schafer)

Sources

Access in a Public Room

The building, the crowd, and who is able to come

Planned

Public settings add conditions that a document-centred approach to accessibility does not reach: sound levels, crowding, sightlines, seating, reach and operating force at an interactive, and the sensory load of a busy gallery. Alongside these sit the conditions that determine attendance at all — admission cost, transport, opening hours and distance — so that scheduled low-sensory times and subsidised access are programme decisions as much as facilities ones.

Grounding. Universal design (Center for Universal Design); accessible physical environments; CRPD Article 30

Sources

  • Reference 15: Connell, B. R., Jones, M., Mace, R., Mueller, J., Mullick, A., Ostroff, E., Sanford, J., Steinfeld, E., Story, M., & Vanderheiden, G. (1997). The Principles of Universal Design, Version 2.0. Center for Universal Design, North Carolina State University.

Access to the Evidence Itself

Whether the reader can open what the work cites

Planned

Practitioners, community organizations and independent researchers frequently work outside the subscriptions their evidence base sits behind. Designing for this means citing what the reader can reach, using open-access versions and repository copies where they exist, and treating access arrangements — community borrower programmes, open licensing, deposit practice — as part of the knowledge product rather than as someone else's concern.

Grounding. Open access (Suber; Budapest Open Access Initiative); public library mandate (IFLA-UNESCO)

Sources

  • Reference 18: Suber, P. (2012). Open Access. MIT Press.
  • Reference 19: Budapest Open Access Initiative (2002). Budapest Open Access Initiative Declaration.

The Reference Encounter as Teaching

One conversation, an unknown starting point, no second session

Planned

A staffed desk in a library or learning centre delivers a large share of an institution's teaching, one enquiry at a time, to people whose prior knowledge is unknown and who may not return. The established reference interview — establishing the actual question, negotiating scope, and closing by checking that the answer was usable — is a compact instructional method, and it pairs with information literacy frameworks to make a single encounter transferable rather than transactional.

Grounding. Reference interview (Ross, Nilsen & Radford); ACRL Framework for Information Literacy

Sources

  • Reference 21: Ross, C. S., Nilsen, K., & Radford, M. L. (2019). Conducting the Reference Interview (3rd ed.). ALA Neal-Schuman.
  • Reference 22: Association of College and Research Libraries (2015). Framework for Information Literacy for Higher Education. American Library Association.

Community-Held Records and Community-Led Digitization

Capability transferred, and the files kept where they belong

Planned

Community archives and digitization initiatives are strongest when the institution supplies equipment, training and standards while the community holds the material, the decisions and the resulting files. Toolkits, workshops and documented workflows are the design deliverables; ownership, control, access and possession of the records remain with the community throughout.

Grounding. OCAP® principles (FNIGC); ethical space (Ermine); Indigenous storywork (Archibald)

Evaluating an Anonymous Encounter

No roster, no follow-up, and evidence all the same

Planned

Public settings rarely permit named participants or later contact, so evaluation is built from what can be observed and asked in the moment: tracking and timing, structured observation, brief exit conversations, and repeat cross-sectional sampling across a season. Each method answers a narrow question well, and stating which question it answers keeps the resulting claims proportionate.

A resource in two areas. Its home is Public Learning, and it is also part of the Evaluation set.

Grounding. Tracking and timing (Serrell); visitor studies methods; informal learning evaluation

Human Rights in Public Interpretation

Rights content in a gallery, a memorial or a public programme

Planned

Public settings reach audiences that no course reaches, and increasingly carry rights content: contested collections, memorials, and exhibitions on histories of harm. The interpretive questions are who narrates, what the visitor is told about provenance, and how a self-directed visitor of any age and background is supported through material of this weight in a short, unsupervised encounter.

A resource in two areas. Its home is Human Rights Education, and it is also part of the Public Learning set.

Grounding. Difficult heritage (Macdonald); interpretation (Serrell); Calls to Action (TRC); museum education (Hooper-Greenhill)
