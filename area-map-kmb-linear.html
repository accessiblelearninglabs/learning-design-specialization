Arts-Based Knowledge Products

Creative form as a route for evidence

Planned

Exhibitions, performance, illustration, film and printed objects reach audiences that reports do not, and can hold complexity that a summary compresses. Designing them as knowledge products means keeping the underlying evidence traceable, stating what the work does and does not claim, and evaluating reach and use as with any other output.

Grounding. Arts-based research practice (Leavy); arts and health evidence (Fancourt & Finn); knowledge product design

Creative Practice as Inquiry with Knowledge Users

Making together as a method of finding out

Planned

Structured making — mapping, model-building, drawing, object work — allows groups to express understanding that interviews reach less readily, and gives participants a shared artefact to reason from. Used within co-production it produces analysable material alongside the relationship, provided consent, authorship and the destination of the work are settled in advance.

Grounding. Arts-based research (Leavy); participatory design (Ehn); integrated knowledge translation; research ethics (TCPS 2)

Evidence Synthesis as a Design Input

Reading, commissioning or conducting the review a design rests on

Planned

Where a design rests on a body of evidence, the quality of that reading sets the limit on what the design can claim. Systematic review method — a stated question, a documented search, applied inclusion criteria, appraisal, and transparent reporting — is what distinguishes a synthesis from a selection. Registering the protocol before the search begins, and reporting to an established standard, lets an independent reader follow the same path and reach the same set of studies.

Grounding. Cochrane Handbook (Higgins et al.); PRISMA 2020 (Page et al.); introduction to systematic reviews (Gough, Oliver & Thomas)

Sources

  • Reference 15: Higgins, J. P. T., Thomas, J., Chandler, J., Cumpston, M., Li, T., Page, M. J., & Welch, V. A. (Eds.). (2019). Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions (2nd ed.). Wiley-Blackwell.
  • Reference 16: Page, M. J., McKenzie, J. E., Bossuyt, P. M., et al. (2021). “The PRISMA 2020 Statement: An Updated Guideline for Reporting Systematic Reviews.” BMJ, 372, n71.
  • Reference 17: Gough, D., Oliver, S., & Thomas, J. (2017). An Introduction to Systematic Reviews (2nd ed.). SAGE Publications.

Spread Beyond the Pilot, and De-implementation

What institutionalizing asks for, and what stopping asks for

Planned

Moving from a successful pilot into routine practice is its own piece of design work: the innovation is specified, the organizations that will hold it are identified, and the budget, policy and training arrangements that sustain it are put in place. The same discipline applies to withdrawal — where a practice has been superseded, an explicit process for removing it, with its own communication and support, keeps the old and the new from running side by side.

Grounding. Scaling-up strategy (WHO & ExpandNet); de-implementation (Prasad & Ioannidis); implementation frameworks (CFIR); exploration and exploitation (March)

Sources
