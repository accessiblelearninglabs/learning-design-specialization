Reading Demand as Diagnosis

What a long queue of requests describes

Planned

A large and persistent volume of learning requests carries information beyond workload. Grouping incoming requests by the underlying situation — rather than by requester or date — surfaces the recurring conditions that people are asking learning to address, and often identifies a small number of structural changes that would reduce demand at its source.

Grounding. Performance analysis (Gilbert; Mager & Pipe); organizational systems (Rummler & Brache); organizational learning (Senge)

Triage When Demand Exceeds Capacity

A published rule, applied consistently

Planned

Where requests will always outnumber capacity, a written and shared allocation rule serves everyone better than sequence of arrival. Tiers of service — full design, consultation, template and self-serve — with stated criteria and expected timelines let requesters plan, make the reasoning reviewable, and give the team a defensible basis for what it takes on.

Grounding. Prioritization and portfolio management; exploration and exploitation (March); service tiering

Where Authority over Learning Sits

Who decides, who advises, and who signs

Planned

Institutions locate curriculum authority differently — in faculty governance, in a professional regulator, in an executive, or in the people delivering the work. Mapping where each decision is actually made, and what a designer can settle, recommend or escalate, allows the work to move without depending on informal influence, and makes handover to a successor straightforward.

A Risk Register for Knowledge-Building Institutions

Conditions to monitor, with indicators attached

Planned

Institutions whose purpose is producing and holding knowledge carry a distinct set of risks: unverified material entering the evidence base, expertise held only in short-term roles, dependence on a single platform or vendor, decay of source records, and funding cycles shorter than the work. Recording these as a register with named indicators and review points allows them to be monitored, in the same way any other institutional risk is.

Grounding. Organizational learning (Senge); complexity and sense-making (Snowden); source and citation integrity

Sources

  • Reference 1: Senge, P. M. (1990). The Fifth Discipline: The Art and Practice of the Learning Organization. Doubleday.

Learning Where Being Wrong Injures Someone

The cluster anchor · design under physical consequence

Planned

In laboratories, workshops, clinical settings, transport and utilities, the cost of an error is borne by a person. Design in these settings works to a different standard: performance demonstrated under realistic conditions rather than knowledge recalled, verification before independent work, and content maintained against the procedure it describes.

Competency Verification Before Authorization

The sign-off that permits independent work

Planned

Authorization to operate equipment, handle material or work unsupervised rests on demonstrated performance, assessed against stated criteria by a named assessor and recorded with a review date. It differs from graded assessment in purpose and in consequence, and the design questions are what counts as sufficient evidence, who is qualified to judge it, and how currency is maintained.

Grounding. Cognitive task analysis (Clark et al.); competency assessment; performance under realistic conditions

Sources

Procedural Drift and What It Calls For

When practice and procedure separate over time

Planned

Practice gradually adapts to conditions the written procedure does not describe, and the adaptation usually succeeds until circumstances change. The documented response is to examine the conditions that made the adaptation reasonable — time pressure, tooling, staffing, a procedure that no longer fits the work — and to revise the procedure or the conditions, with instruction addressing what remains.

Grounding. Normalization of deviance (Vaughan); organizational accidents (Reason); understanding human error (Dekker)

Sources

  • Reference 24: Vaughan, D. (1996). The Challenger Launch Decision: Risky Technology, Culture, and Deviance at NASA. University of Chicago Press.
  • Reference 18: Reason, J. (1997). Managing the Risks of Organizational Accidents. Ashgate.
  • Reference 25: Dekker, S. (2014). The Field Guide to Understanding ‘Human Error’ (3rd ed.). Ashgate.

Designing for Error Tolerance

Support at the point of action, not recall under pressure

Planned

Where consequence is high, the reliable designs reduce what has to be remembered: checklists, forcing functions, sequenced prompts, clear labelling and equipment that resists being assembled incorrectly. Instruction then covers what these cannot — judgement, recognition of the unfamiliar, and the decision to stop — which is a smaller and more teachable set.

Grounding. Human error models (Reason); checklists (Gawande); job aids (Rossett & Schafer); design affordances (Norman)

Sources

  • Reference 21: Reason, J. (2000). “Human Error: Models and Management.” BMJ, 320(7237), 768–770.
  • Reference 26: Gawande, A. (2009). The Checklist Manifesto: How to Get Things Right. Metropolitan Books.

Incidents and Near-Misses as Design Inputs

What the record already contains

Planned

Incident reports, near-miss logs and safety reviews are among the most specific accounts an organization holds of where practice meets difficulty. Reading them as design evidence identifies which situations recur and which supports would help, and the value of the record depends on reporting being straightforward and being seen to lead to change.

Teaching Technical Experts in Regulated Environments

Specialist knowledge, a regulator, and a shared record

Planned

Research facilities, utilities and clinical services combine deep specialist expertise with external oversight and a training record that must satisfy a reviewer. The craft is drawing out what an expert knows procedurally, keeping the material accurate as procedures are revised, and producing evidence that is genuinely useful to the people doing the work as well as complete for audit.

Grounding. Cognitive task analysis (Clark et al.); working with subject-matter experts; regulatory training records

Sources

  • Reference 22: Clark, R. E., Feldon, D. F., van Merriënboer, J. J. G., Yates, K. A., & Early, S. (2008). “Cognitive Task Analysis.” In Handbook of Research on Educational Communications and Technology (3rd ed.). Routledge.

The Workplace Partner Who Does Most of the Teaching

Supervisors as the largest and least supported teaching group

Planned

In placements, co-ops and practicums the day-to-day teaching is done by workplace supervisors whose primary role is something else. Supporting them well is a design problem in its own right: a short and specific supervisor brief, a small number of structured moments in the placement, worked examples of useful feedback, and a named contact when a situation exceeds the brief.

A resource in two areas. Its home is Learning Experience Design, and it is also part of the Learning Systems Design set.

Grounding. Work-integrated learning (Billett; CEWIL Canada); building design capability in non-designers
