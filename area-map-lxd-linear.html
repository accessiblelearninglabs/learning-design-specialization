Section 1

About this branch

The design of learning as it is actually experienced — how a learner moves through it, engages with it, and makes it their own. This branch gathers the contributions that shape the experience itself, from motivation through practice, reflection, and the journey that holds them together.

The five-phase rhythm — Why · See · Try · Share · Reflect

Published resource

The end-to-end arc a learner moves through, designed as one coherent journey rather than a sequence of modules. It is the organizing spine of the experience work, and the frame every other contribution on this branch extends.

Designing for Motivation & Relevance

Why adult learners engage — or quietly disengage

In development

Anchoring learning to a learner's real circumstances and existing expertise before content is introduced, so relevance and autonomy carry the motivational work. It addresses the most common reason programs fall short: attention and completion that quietly decline.

Grounding. Self-Determination Theory (Ryan & Deci)

Sources

  • Reference 3: Ryan, R. M., & Deci, E. L. (2000). “Self-Determination Theory and the Facilitation of Intrinsic Motivation, Social Development, and Well-Being.” American Psychologist, 55(1), 68–78.

Managing Cognitive Load

Learning that working memory can hold

In development

Chunking, sequencing, worked examples, and multimedia principles that keep dense material within the limits of attention and memory. It matters most where content is genuinely complex, as in research and health settings.

Grounding. Cognitive Load Theory (Sweller); Multimedia Learning (Mayer)

Sources

  • Reference 4: Sweller, J. (1988). “Cognitive Load During Problem Solving: Effects on Learning.” Cognitive Science, 12(2), 257–285. See also Sweller, Ayres & Kalyuga, Cognitive Load Theory (Springer, 2011).
  • Reference 5: Mayer, R. E. (2009). Multimedia Learning (2nd ed.). Cambridge University Press.

Experiential Learning Infrastructure

Learning by doing, built to last

Published resource

The conditions and structures that let adult learners practise, make consequential mistakes, and build capability through experience rather than exposure. It treats error as a designed source of feedback, not a failure of instruction.

A resource in two areas. Its home is Learning Experience Design, and it is also part of the Learning Systems Design set.

Designing for Reflection & Transfer

Making learning adapt to new situations

In development

Reflection and metacognition designed in, so learners can make what they have learned their own and re-deploy it in situations the designer could not anticipate. It is the experience-level form of closing the distance between knowing and doing.

Grounding. Metacognition (Flavell); reflective practice (Schön); Bransford et al.

Backward Design in Practice

Aligning outcomes, assessment, and activity

In development

Holding a single learning experience coherent by starting from the intended outcome, then the evidence of it, then the activity — so nothing in the experience is arbitrary. It answers a recurring need in program and accreditation contexts.

Grounding. Backward design (Wiggins & McTighe)

Studio and Workshop Pedagogy

Teaching in a space where the work is made

Planned

Learning organized around production in a shared, equipped space: demonstration, supervised practice, progressive release of independence, and authorization to work unaccompanied once competence is shown. It is among the oldest teaching structures in the professions, and the reflective practicum was theorised from it, yet little of its method has been carried into mainstream design practice.

Grounding. Reflective practicum (Schön); signature pedagogies (Shulman); cognitive apprenticeship (Collins, Brown & Newman); craft (Sennett)

Sources

The Critique as an Assessment Form

Public, spoken feedback, designed well

Planned

The critique gives feedback in front of peers, in the presence of the work, and develops the ability to discuss one's own practice — something no written rubric produces. Designing it well means stating the criteria beforehand, structuring who speaks and in what order, protecting time so later work is heard as fully as earlier work, and providing a written record so the session is usable afterwards.

Grounding. Art and design pedagogy (Orr & Shreeve); feedback (Hattie & Timperley); construct-irrelevant barriers (Messick)

Sources

  • Reference 21: Orr, S., & Shreeve, A. (2018). Art and Design Pedagogy in Higher Education: Knowledge, Values and Ambiguity in the Creative Curriculum. Routledge.
  • Reference 15: Hattie, J., & Timperley, H. (2007). “The Power of Feedback.” Review of Educational Research, 77(1), 81–112.
  • Reference 22: Messick, S. (1995). “Validity of Psychological Assessment: Validation of Inferences from Persons’ Responses and Performances as Scientific Inquiry into Score Meaning.” American Psychologist, 50(9), 741–749.

Materials, Tools and Equipment as Access Variables

What the work costs to make, and who can operate the machine

Planned

In making-based programmes the material budget, the studio timetable and the physical demands of equipment shape who can fully take part. Costed material kits, consumable pools, timetabled access that accommodates commuting and caregiving, and equipment selected for reach, force and seated operation are the design responses, and they belong in the programme plan rather than in individual accommodation.

Grounding. Universal design (Center for Universal Design); scarcity and cognitive bandwidth (Mullainathan & Shafir); accessible environments

Sources

  • Reference 23: Connell, B. R., Jones, M., Mace, R., Mueller, J., Mullick, A., Ostroff, E., Sanford, J., Steinfeld, E., Story, M., & Vanderheiden, G. (1997). The Principles of Universal Design, Version 2.0. Center for Universal Design, North Carolina State University.
  • Reference 24: Mullainathan, S., & Shafir, E. (2013). Scarcity: Why Having Too Little Means So Much. Times Books.
  • Reference 25: CAST (2024). Universal Design for Learning Guidelines, version 3.0.

Designing For and Within Practice-Based Research

When the work made is part of the finding

Planned

Research-creation and practice-led inquiry produce knowledge through making, which changes how a project is scoped, supervised, documented and examined. Designing learning in these settings means building documentation into production rather than reconstructing it afterwards, and making the criteria for a practice-based contribution explicit to everyone assessing it.

A resource in two areas. Its home is Learning Experience Design, and it is also part of the Epistemology set.

Grounding. Practice as research (Barrett & Bolt); knowing-how (Ryle); tacit knowledge (Polanyi; Collins)

Sources

Partnered Studio and Client Projects

An external partner, a live brief, and learners in the middle

Planned

Projects run with an industry, community or institutional partner give learners real constraints and real audiences. Holding the educational purpose steady alongside the partner's needs calls for an agreement settled in advance: scope and timeline, ownership of what is produced, consent and credit for participants, and a defined position if the brief changes mid-project.

Grounding. Work-integrated learning (Billett); situated learning; research ethics (TCPS 2)

Sources

  • Reference 30: Billett, S. (2009). “Realising the Educational Worth of Integrating Work Experiences in Higher Education.” Studies in Higher Education, 34(7), 827–843.

The Workplace Partner Who Does Most of the Teaching

Supervisors as the largest and least supported teaching group

Planned

In placements, co-ops and practicums the day-to-day teaching is done by workplace supervisors whose primary role is something else. Supporting them well is a design problem in its own right: a short and specific supervisor brief, a small number of structured moments in the placement, worked examples of useful feedback, and a named contact when a situation exceeds the brief.

A resource in two areas. Its home is Learning Experience Design, and it is also part of the Learning Systems Design set.

Grounding. Work-integrated learning (Billett; CEWIL Canada); building design capability in non-designers

Sources

Placement Quality as a Design Variable

What distinguishes a strong placement from a filled one

Planned

Placement quality can be specified rather than hoped for: identified learning outcomes, graduated responsibility, scheduled feedback, exposure to more than one task, and a documented review at the midpoint while the term can still be adjusted. Making these explicit at the point of agreement gives every party the same expectations and gives the institution something to review against.

Grounding. Work-integrated learning quality principles (CEWIL Canada); Billett; constructive alignment (Biggs)

Sources

  • Reference 32: Co-operative Education and Work-Integrated Learning Canada (CEWIL Canada). Work-Integrated Learning definitions and quality principles.
  • Reference 30: Billett, S. (2009). “Realising the Educational Worth of Integrating Work Experiences in Higher Education.” Studies in Higher Education, 34(7), 827–843.
  • Reference 33: Biggs, J. (1996). “Enhancing Teaching Through Constructive Alignment.” Higher Education, 32(3), 347–364.

Who Is Able to Take the Placement

Unpaid and unfunded experience as an access question

Planned

Where placement is required for a credential and unpaid or unfunded, participation depends on financial circumstance, caregiving, transport and immigration status as much as on capability. Paid and funded placements, part-time and local options, remote arrangements and clear accommodation routes widen who can complete the requirement, and tracking who withdraws makes the pattern visible.

Grounding. Unpaid internships and access (Perlin); placement access (Meeks & Jain); equity-centred design

Sources

  • Reference 34: Perlin, R. (2011). Intern Nation: How to Earn Nothing and Learn Little in the Brave New Economy. Verso.
  • Reference 35: Meeks, L. M., & Jain, N. R. (2018). Accessibility, Inclusion, and Action in Medical Education: Lived Experiences of Learners and Physicians with Disabilities. Association of American Medical Colleges.
  • Reference 36: Ladson-Billings, G. (1995). “Toward a Theory of Culturally Relevant Pedagogy.” American Educational Research Journal, 32(3), 465–491.
