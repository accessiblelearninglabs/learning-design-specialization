Section 1

About this branch

Access understood at the level of the individual person and the obligation an institution holds toward them, alongside the proactive design that reduces how often that obligation must be invoked. This branch gathers knowledge resources written for learning designers, faculty, and institutions, offered in support of — never in place of — the accessibility advisors and accommodation professionals who carry this work day to day.

Grounding. Human rights and accommodation law; disability studies; accessibility standards; inclusive design

Why Universal Design Never Eliminates Individual Accommodation

The anchor · why both are permanent, and both are skilled

In development

Individual accommodation is not a residue left behind by imperfect design. Human variability has no outer edge, some barriers are the very thing an assessment is meant to measure, and the duty is owed to a person rather than discharged by a process — which is why proactive design and individual accommodation remain complementary, and why the accommodation encounter is skilled professional practice rather than an administrative step.

Grounding. Inclusive design and one-size-fits-one; the duty to accommodate; construct validity

The Duty to Accommodate in Learning Design

The legal frame, written for those who build and teach

In development

The architecture within which access decisions are made and defended: the procedural and substantive duty, the unified bona fide requirement test, and accommodation to the point of undue hardship, set in the Canadian frame with the cross-border standards that vendor and partnership work require. It is offered as synthesis for designers, faculty, and institutions; determinations in individual cases rest with accessibility advisors and accommodation offices, and with legal counsel where a matter becomes contested.

Grounding. Meiorin and the BFOR test; Accessible Canada Act; BC Human Rights Code; Accessible British Columbia Act; ADA and Sections 504/508

Sources

Essential Requirements and the Limits of Flexibility

What a standard genuinely requires, articulated in advance

In development

The most demanding judgment in the field: when flexibility preserves what is being measured, and when it alters it. This resource supports programs in articulating their essential requirements before any dispute arises — separating what a standard genuinely requires from what it has inherited by convention — so that the reasoning is available to everyone who later needs it.

Disability Studies for Learning Designers

The grounding that changes what a designer sees

In development

Social, medical, and interactional models of disability; ableism as institutional structure rather than individual attitude; disability as identity, culture, and political community. It is the grounding that allows a barrier to be named as a design failure rather than a learner’s deficit, and it is what distinguishes accessibility practice from compliance administration.

Designing Out the Disclosure Requirement

Disclosure burden as a design variable

In development

Access that is gated behind disclosure asks a person to name a private matter to someone holding authority over them, and the documented costs of that request are real: anticipated stigma, delay, and for some, a decision not to ask at all. This resource treats disclosure burden as something design can measure and reduce — why a flexible deadline requiring an email is a disclosure event and a built-in buffer is not — while leaving the accommodation record itself where it belongs, with the office that holds it.

Grounding. Stigma and concealment (Goffman); academic ableism; BC information-governance requirements

Sources

  • Reference 15: Goffman, E. (1963). Stigma: Notes on the Management of Spoiled Identity. Prentice-Hall.
  • Reference 12: Dolmage, J. T. (2017). Academic Ableism: Disability and Higher Education. University of Michigan Press.
  • Reference 16: Freedom of Information and Protection of Privacy Act, R.S.B.C. 1996, c. 165. Province of British Columbia.

Designing for the Assistive Technology People Actually Use

Why conformant content can still fail in practice

In development

This resource covers testing with actual assistive technology rather than automated checking alone, and the reasons technically conformant material can still obstruct — while leaving assessment, tool matching, and training to the assistive technology specialists and advisors whose professional work that is.

Program architecture for conditions that vary

In development

Attendance policy, deadline architecture, cohort progression, and leave-and-return pathways — the structural layer of a problem that individual accommodation handles with difficulty because most systems assume stable limitations. Models of Disability as Design Decisions

The frame chosen before any design decision is made

In development

Medical, social, biopsychosocial, economic, functional solutions, social identity or cultural affiliation, and charity — seven models in active circulation, each of which settles in advance what counts as the problem and who is obliged to act on it. This resource sets them beside one another with their strengths and their costs, and treats the choice of model as a design decision rather than a philosophical preference, because the model implicit in a project brief is usually the model that shapes what gets built.

Grounding. ICF (WHO, 2001); social model (Oliver); Retief & Letšosa; Shakespeare

Sources

Disability Categories and the Barriers People Meet

The reference layer, written barrier-first

In development

Visual, auditory, deafblind, speech, mobility and body structure, cognitive and learning, seizure, and psychological disabilities, each set against the barriers people actually encounter in digital and physical environments. It is written barrier-first rather than diagnosis-first, so that a designer reaches for the environmental change rather than the label — and it is the reference layer that the more argumentative resources on this branch quietly assume a reader already holds.

Disability Data and the Limits of Prevalence Figures

Why the numbers disagree, and how to use one honestly

In development

Measurement by diagnosis, by function, or by self-identification produces materially different populations, which is why global and national prevalence figures diverge so widely and why the choice of instrument is never neutral. This resource covers the major global and Canadian sources and their known undercounts, and how to carry a number into a business case or a funding application without asking it to bear more weight than it can.

In development
