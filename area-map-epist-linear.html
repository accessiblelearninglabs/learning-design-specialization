Section 1

About this area

The conditions under which a knowledge claim is admitted, discounted, or never formed at all — the questions that arise before evidence is appraised rather than during it. This branch gathers the contributions that give a design process language for how it decides what will count as knowledge, and for what the exclusions cost. It supplies analysis; the standing obligations owed to communities and to Indigenous knowledge holders remain with the branches and instruments that carry them.

Grounding. Social epistemology; epistemic injustice; science and technology studies; socio-legal epistemology of evidence

These resources are planned rather than published. They are listed so the shape of the branch is legible in full, and so what is still to come is as visible as what is finished.

A resource belongs on this branch only where the failure it addresses happens before evidence is appraised rather than during it; appraisal itself belongs to the shared craft of Learning Design. The branch coordinates with the Indigenous-led resources held on the Knowledge Mobilization branch and does not contain them.

What a Sound Knowledge Process Looks Like

The entry node · the standard the rest of the branch measures against

Planned

Four conditions rendered for design work: real avenues through which criticism can travel while a decision can still change, demonstrable uptake of that criticism, standards of evidence agreed before evidence is gathered, and authority that tracks relevant knowledge rather than rank. They are necessary rather than sufficient — they make error detectable, not absent — and the fourth cannot be self-certified, which is why it is written as an arrangement a process submits to rather than a check it performs on itself.

Grounding. Critical contextual empiricism (Longino); strong objectivity (Harding); veritistic social epistemology (Goldman)

Sources

  • Reference 1: Longino, H. E. (2002). The Fate of Knowledge. Princeton University Press.
  • Reference 2: Harding, S. (1986). The Science Question in Feminism. Cornell University Press.
  • Reference 3: Goldman, A. I. (1999). Knowledge in a Social World. Oxford University Press.

When Testimony Becomes Evidence

The criterion an evidence hierarchy cannot supply

Planned

What makes a firsthand account count as evidence, and equally what makes a claim fail regardless of who holds it. It is the contribution that keeps a widened set of knowers from becoming an unlimited set of claims, and it leaves appraisal discipline — distinguishing a robust finding from folklore — fully intact.

Grounding. Epistemology of testimony (Coady; Lackey); epistemic dependence (Hardwig); testimonial injustice (Fricker)

Sources

The Invisible Rules of Admissibility

The diagnostic · specifiable rules rather than bias

Planned

Sequencing, genre, the credential gate, proxy substitution, small-n erasure, safety closure, and the upstream lock: named filters that settle what enters a record before anything in it is weighed. Naming them as rules rather than as bias is what makes them examinable, and correctable, by the room that has just applied one.

Grounding. Epistemic injustice (Fricker; Dotson); socio-legal work on admissibility, expert qualification, and burden of proof

Sources

Knowledge That Was Never Produced

The absence that leaves no trace

Planned

Questions never funded, populations never studied, outcomes never defined. Nothing was excluded because nothing was submitted, which makes non-production the likeliest reason an evidence base looks thin and the hardest thing to detect from inside a literature search.

Grounding. Agnotology (Proctor & Schiebinger); structured ignorance (Mills); Tuana

Sources

  • Reference 12: Proctor, R. N., & Schiebinger, L. (Eds.). (2008). Agnotology: The Making and Unmaking of Ignorance. Stanford University Press.

Experience Without Language

Hermeneutical injustice · when the account cannot be formed

Planned

Nobody is disbelieved and no claim is rejected: the concept required to state the experience does not yet exist in the shared interpretive resource. It leaves no trace in a consultation record, so a process that measures only what was said will read it as an absence of concern.

Grounding. Hermeneutical and contributory injustice (Fricker; Dotson); idioms of distress (Nichter); Kleinman

Making as Knowing

Whether knowing-how is knowledge in the same sense

Planned

The form of knowledge most present in craft, facilitation, and clinical practice, and least legible to a hierarchy built for claims. Elicitation methods presuppose an answer to this question and rarely state it, which is the point at which a tacit account is either carried into a design or lost at the interview.

Grounding. Knowing-how (Ryle); tacit knowledge (Polanyi; H. Collins); reflective practice (Schön)

Sources

  • Reference 17: Ryle, G. (1949). The Concept of Mind. Hutchinson — the source of the knowing-how and knowing-that distinction.
  • Reference 18: Polanyi, M. (1958). Personal Knowledge: Towards a Post-Critical Philosophy. University of Chicago Press.
  • Reference 19: Collins, H. (2010). Tacit and Explicit Knowledge. University of Chicago Press.
  • Reference 20: Schön, D. A. (1983). The Reflective Practitioner: How Professionals Think in Action. Basic Books.

Warranted Trust and the Division of Cognitive Labour

When deference is well-grounded rather than deferential

Planned

Nobody verifies most of what they know, so dependence is the ordinary condition of expertise rather than a lapse in it. This contribution sets out the grounds on which trust in an expert, a source, or a community is warranted, alongside the documented cases in which expertise established outside formal credentials has held up against expertise inside them.

Grounding. Epistemic dependence (Hardwig); epistemology of expertise (Goldman); interactional expertise (Collins & Evans); civic epistemology (Jasanoff)

Sources

Designing Under Disagreement

Deciding and building when knowledgeable people conflict

Planned

Disagreement between well-informed people working inside a single framework, where a decision still has to be made and something still has to be built to a deadline. It covers what the disagreement is itself evidence of, and which deliberative arrangements produce a defensible decision rather than the loudest one.

Grounding. Peer disagreement (Feldman & Warfield); deliberative method (Fishkin; Landemore); participatory design (Ehn)

Sources

When Integration Becomes Assimilation

Where synthesis is the wrong instrument

Planned

Two knowledge systems can answer the same question incompatibly, and integrating them under unequal power absorbs one into the other. Holding the conflict open is an arrangement to be maintained rather than a deliverable to be produced. This contribution coordinates with the Indigenous-led work held elsewhere on the map and does not stand in for it.

Grounding. Ethical space (Ermine); Two-Eyed Seeing (Bartlett, Marshall & Marshall); legal pluralism

The Construct Inside the Measure

How an instrument decides which outcomes become visible

Planned

An instrument encodes a construct, and choosing it settles in advance what will be able to register as an outcome. Where the construct does not hold in the setting, the measure returns nothing on something that worked, or an effect on something nobody intended.

Grounding. Construct validity (Messick; Borsboom et al.); category fallacy (Kleinman); idioms of distress (Nichter)

The Designer’s Own Position

The assumptions a method cannot catch on its own

Planned

No method examines the assumptions it was built on, and elicitation presumes a neutral extractor. The correction is criticism from a differently positioned knower holding authority to overrule — an arrangement submitted to rather than a process run — which applies to the designer the same condition a sound knowledge process is asked to meet.

Grounding. Strong objectivity (Harding); situated knowledges (Haraway); Wylie; Hill Collins; Longino

Sources

  • Reference 2: Harding, S. (1986). The Science Question in Feminism. Cornell University Press.
  • Reference 33: Haraway, D. (1988). “Situated Knowledges: The Science Question in Feminism and the Privilege of Partial Perspective.” Feminist Studies, 14(3), 575–599.
  • Reference 34: Wylie, A. (2003). “Why Standpoint Matters.” In R. Figueroa & S. Harding (Eds.), Science and Other Cultures: Issues in Philosophies of Science and Technology. Routledge.
